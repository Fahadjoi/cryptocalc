How the profit calculation works

A trade has three money flows: what the position is worth when you open it, what it is worth when you close it, and the fees the exchange takes at both ends. Profit is the difference between the first two, minus the third.

Net profit (long) = coins × exit price − position size − entry fee − exit feeCoins = position size ÷ entry price. Position size = your money × leverage. For a short, the first two terms swap places.

The entry fee is charged on the position size and the exit fee on the value at exit, so a winning trade pays a slightly larger exit fee than a losing one. Many calculators charge both fees on the entry value, which understates the cost of a profitable trade.

A worked example

You put $1,000 into Bitcoin at $60,000 and sell at $66,000, paying 0.1% each way.

Coins bought: 1,000 ÷ 60,000 = 0.016667 BTC. Value at exit: 0.016667 × 66,000 = $1,100.

Fees: $1.00 going in and $1.10 coming out. Net profit: 1,100 − 1,000 − 2.10 = $97.90, a 9.79% return on a 10% price move.

Break-even price

Because of fees, selling at your entry price loses money. The break-even price is the exit at which the two fees are exactly covered.

Break-even (long) = entry × (1 + entry fee) ÷ (1 − exit fee)

At 0.1% each way that is about 0.2% above entry. It sounds small, but a scalper aiming for 0.5% moves gives up two fifths of every win to it. On a short the break-even sits the same distance below entry.

Leverage, margin and liquidation

Leverage lets a small deposit control a larger position. With $1,000 at 10× you hold a $10,000 position, so a 1% price move changes your balance by about 10%. Fees are charged on the full $10,000 as well, which is why leveraged trades pay ten times the fees of the same deposit on spot.

The exchange closes the position when losses use up your margin. This calculator estimates that level as:

Liquidation (long) ≈ entry × (1 − 1 ÷ leverage + 0.5%)For a short: entry × (1 + 1 ÷ leverage − 0.5%). Isolated margin with a 0.5% maintenance margin rate.

The 0.5% is the maintenance margin most exchanges keep back on large coins at small sizes. Yours may differ, and the liquidation calculator lets you set it exactly. If the exit you typed lies beyond liquidation, the result shows the loss of your full margin, because the trade would never reach that price.

Funding on perpetual futures

Perpetual contracts have no expiry, so exchanges use a funding payment to keep their price near the spot price. Every eight hours one side pays the other a small percentage of the position. When the rate is positive, longs pay shorts. When it is negative, shorts pay longs.

Funding = position size × rate × 3 × days heldThree payments a day. Paid by longs and received by shorts when the rate is positive.

A typical rate of 0.01% looks harmless, but on a $10,000 position held for 30 days it comes to $90, more than four times the trading fees on the same trade. Rates change at every payment, so use an average for the period you expect to hold.

Long and short

A long buys first and sells later, so it profits when the price rises. A short sells borrowed coins first and buys them back later, so it profits when the price falls. Short profit is capped, because a price cannot fall below zero, while a short's loss is not capped if the price keeps rising. Spot accounts can only go long. Shorts need a margin or futures account.

What this calculator leaves out

  • Slippage, the gap between the price you see and the price your order fills at, which grows with order size and thin order books.
  • Withdrawal and network fees for moving coins off the exchange.
  • Tax on the gain, which depends on where you live.

Common questions

How do I calculate crypto profit?

Divide the money you put in by the buy price to get the number of coins, multiply the coins by the sell price, then subtract what you put in and the fees for both trades. With $1,000 bought at $60,000 and sold at $66,000 at 0.1% fees, the profit is $97.90.

Why is my profit lower than the price change suggests?

Fees are taken when you buy and again when you sell. A 10% price rise with 0.1% fees on each side gives about 9.79%, and with 0.6% fees about 8.74%.

Does leverage increase the fees I pay?

Yes. Fees are charged on the whole position, not on your deposit. A $1,000 deposit at 10× leverage pays fees on $10,000, so the same percentage fee costs ten times as much.

How accurate is the liquidation price?

It is an estimate for isolated margin that ignores maintenance margin, funding and fees. Your exchange will liquidate slightly earlier than the figure shown, so always check the liquidation price on the order screen before opening a position.

Can I use this for coins that are not in the list?

Yes. Pick any coin and type your own entry and exit prices. The coin choice only controls which live price the Live buttons fill in.
