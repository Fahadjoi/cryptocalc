What liquidation is

When you trade with leverage, the exchange lends you most of the position and holds your own money, the margin, as security. If the price moves against you far enough that your margin can no longer cover the loss, the exchange closes the position itself to get its loan back. That forced close is liquidation, and it usually costs you the whole margin for that position.

The formula

Liquidation (long) = entry × (1 − 1 ÷ leverage + maintenance margin rate)Liquidation (short) = entry × (1 + 1 ÷ leverage − maintenance margin rate). For isolated margin on USDT-margined contracts.

The term 1 ÷ leverage is how far the price can move before your margin is entirely used up. At 10× that is 10%. The exchange does not wait that long. It keeps a safety buffer called the maintenance margin and liquidates when your remaining margin falls to that level, which is why the maintenance rate is added back for a long and subtracted for a short.

A worked example

You open a 10× long at $60,000 with $1,000 of margin, so the position is $10,000. The maintenance margin rate is 0.5%.

Liquidation: 60,000 × (1 − 0.10 + 0.005) = 60,000 × 0.905 = $54,300.

A fall of 9.5% closes the position. Without the maintenance buffer it would have taken the full 10%, down to $54,000.

How leverage changes the room you have

The table in the calculator shows the same entry at different leverage. The pattern is simple: doubling the leverage roughly halves the distance to liquidation. At 5× a coin has to move nearly 20% against you. At 50× it takes less than 2%, a move that large coins make in an ordinary hour.

Leverage does not make a trade more profitable in dollar terms for the same position size. It only lowers the margin you post, and brings the liquidation price closer. If your stop-loss sits beyond the liquidation price, the stop will never trigger. The position size calculator checks that for you.

Isolated and cross margin

Isolated margin ties a fixed amount to one position. The most you can lose is that amount, and the liquidation price is the one calculated here. Cross margin lets the position draw on your whole futures balance, which pushes liquidation further away but puts the entire balance at risk. Under cross margin the liquidation price also moves as your other positions gain or lose, so no simple calculator can pin it down.

Ways to keep liquidation away

  • Lower the leverage. The most direct fix, and it costs nothing but margin.
  • Add margin to an open isolated position. This moves the liquidation price further from the market.
  • Set a stop-loss before liquidation. A stop closes the trade at a loss you chose, and you keep the remaining margin.
  • Size the position smaller. A smaller position with the same margin is the same thing as lower leverage.

What this calculator leaves out

  • Funding payments, which are taken from margin every few hours on perpetual contracts and slowly pull liquidation closer on a losing side.
  • Tiered maintenance rates. Exchanges raise the rate for larger positions. Use the rate for your size.
  • Liquidation fees and the mark price. Exchanges liquidate on a mark price, an average across markets, which can differ slightly from the last traded price you see.
  • Coin-margined contracts, which use a different formula.

Common questions

How do I calculate my liquidation price?

For an isolated long, multiply the entry price by (1 − 1 ÷ leverage + maintenance margin rate). For a short, multiply by (1 + 1 ÷ leverage − maintenance margin rate). A 10× long at $60,000 with a 0.5% maintenance rate is liquidated at $54,300.

Do I lose all my money when I am liquidated?

With isolated margin you lose the margin assigned to that position, less any small remainder the exchange returns. With cross margin the loss can reach your whole futures balance.

What is maintenance margin?

It is the minimum margin the exchange requires you to keep in a position, set as a percentage of the position size. When your margin falls to that level the position is liquidated. Typical rates start around 0.4% to 0.5% for major coins.

Why is my exchange showing a different liquidation price?

Exchanges include fees, funding already paid, tiered maintenance rates and, under cross margin, your other balances. Treat this figure as a close estimate and always use the price shown on your order screen.

Can a 1× position be liquidated?

A 1× long would only be liquidated if the price fell almost to zero. A 1× short is liquidated if the price roughly doubles, because the loss then equals your margin.
