The right way to average

Average cost = total money spent ÷ total coins bought

Adding the prices together and dividing by the number of buys only works when every buy was for the same number of coins. If you spent the same amount of money each time, the cheaper buys collected more coins and pull the average down.

You spend $500 on Bitcoin at $60,000 and another $500 at $40,000.

Coins: 0.008333 + 0.0125 = 0.020833 BTC

Average cost: 1,000 ÷ 0.020833 = $48,000, not $50,000

If instead you had bought 0.01 BTC each time, you would have spent $600 and then $400, and the average would be exactly $50,000. Same prices, different answer, because the amounts differ.

What averaging down does

Averaging down means buying more after the price has fallen. It lowers the average cost, so the price needs to recover less before the whole position breaks even.

You hold 0.02 BTC bought at $60,000, a cost of $1,200. The price is now $45,000.

You buy another $900, which is 0.02 BTC.

New average: 2,100 ÷ 0.04 = $52,500

Before the second buy, the price had to climb 33% to get back to break-even. After it, the climb is 16.7%.

How much to buy for a target average

Extra coins = coins held × (current average − target) ÷ (target − price now)

Same holding: 0.02 BTC at an average of $60,000, with the price at $45,000. You want an average of $50,000.

Extra coins: 0.02 × (60,000 − 50,000) ÷ (50,000 − 45,000) = 0.04 BTC, costing $1,800

The closer the target is to the current price, the more it costs. An average equal to the current price can never be reached, because every coin you already hold cost more than that.

What it does not do

  • It does not reduce the loss you already have. The first 0.02 BTC is still worth $300 less than you paid for it.
  • It increases what is at risk. In the example the money in the coin went from $1,200 to $2,100. If the price keeps falling, the loss now grows faster.
  • It does not make a weak coin recover. Averaging down helps only if the price comes back, so the question to settle first is whether you would buy this coin today with fresh money.

Getting an accurate figure

Include fees in the money spent, and use the coins that actually arrived after the fee. For buys made on a schedule, the same weighted sum applies. That is the mechanism behind dollar-cost averaging.
