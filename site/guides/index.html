Guides

The sums behind a trade, explained with worked examples you can check by hand. Each guide links to the calculator that does the same maths with live prices.

  • How to calculate crypto profit after feesThe price moved 10% in your favour, yet the balance grew by less. Here is where the difference goes and how to work it out before you trade.
  • Break-even price: how far a coin must move to cover feesSelling at the price you bought at is a loss, because you paid a fee going in and pay another coming out. The break-even price is the first price at which you get all your money back.
  • Position sizing: how much to buy on each tradeMost traders pick a size first and a stop second. Doing it the other way round is what keeps one bad trade from deciding the month.
  • Risk-reward ratio and the win rate you needA trader who is right 35% of the time can make money, and one who is right 70% of the time can lose it. The ratio between the average win and the average loss decides which.
  • How liquidation price works on leveraged tradesWith leverage, the exchange lends you most of the position. Liquidation is the price at which your own share is nearly gone and the exchange closes the trade to protect its loan.
  • Isolated vs cross marginThe margin mode is one switch on the order form, and it decides whether a bad trade can lose only its own stake or the whole futures balance.
  • Average cost and averaging downAfter a few buys at different prices, the number that matters is the one price at which the whole holding breaks even. It is rarely the middle of the prices you paid.
  • Maker and taker fees explainedTwo people can place the same size order on the same exchange and pay different fees. The difference comes from how the order reaches the market.
  • Funding rates on perpetual futuresA perpetual future never expires, so something has to keep its price close to the real coin. That something is funding, a payment that passes between longs and shorts several times a day.
  • Dollar-cost averaging vs lump sumBuying a fixed amount on a schedule takes the timing decision away. Whether it also earns more than buying all at once depends on what the price did next.
  • APR vs APY in stakingTwo platforms can advertise 10% and pay different amounts. One is quoting the rate before compounding and the other after it.
  • Why you get less than the converter showsA converter shows the mid-market rate, the midpoint between what buyers bid and sellers ask. Nobody trades at exactly that price, and four separate costs sit between it and the money that reaches you.
  • How much you need to gain to recover from a lossLosses and gains are not symmetrical. Lose half of a portfolio and the half that is left has to double just to get back to where you started.
  • How leverage works in crypto tradingLeverage does not change how much the price moves. It changes how much of your own money each move is worth, in both directions.
  • Market cap maths: what price a coin needs for a given market cap"Can this coin reach $100?" has an answer you can work out in one line. The answer depends on how many coins exist, not just on the price.
  • Crypto cost basis: FIFO, specific ID and average costSell one coin out of several you bought at different prices and the gain depends on which purchase that coin is said to come from. The method decides, and in some countries the law decides the method.
  • How to set a stop lossA stop loss is the price where you accept the trade was wrong. Set it from the chart first, then size the position to fit it, never the other way round.
  • Taking profit in stagesSelling everything at one target is a single bet on where the price stops. Selling in parts locks some profit in early and keeps some of the position for a bigger move.
  • Spot vs futures trading in cryptoOn spot you buy the coin. On futures you hold a contract that tracks the coin's price. That difference decides whether you can be liquidated, what you pay to hold, and whether you can profit from a fall.
  • Slippage and spreadThe price on the screen is not quite the price you get. The spread and slippage are costs on every market order, on top of the trading fee.
  • Percentage gain, ROI and annual return"Up 200%" can mean very different things depending on fees, time and where you started counting. Three short formulas cover almost every case.
  • Win rate and expectancyA high win rate does not make a strategy profitable, and a low one does not make it a loser. What matters is how much you make on the winners compared with what you lose on the losers.
  • Limit vs market ordersA market order buys now at whatever price is available. A limit order waits for your price. The choice changes the fee you pay, the price you get and whether you get filled at all.
  • Trailing stop lossA trailing stop moves up as the price rises and stays put when it falls. It lets a winning trade keep running while making sure most of the gain is kept if the trend turns.
  • Hedging your coins with a shortIf you want to keep your coins but not their price risk for a while, you can short the same amount on a futures market. Gains on one side cover losses on the other.
  • Losing streaks and risk of ruinEvery strategy has losing runs. The amount you risk on each trade decides whether a bad run is an annoyance or the end of the account.
  • Bitcoin supply and the halvingThere will only ever be 21 million bitcoin, and the rate at which new ones appear halves roughly every four years. The numbers behind that are simpler than they sound.
  • Realised vs unrealised profitUnrealised profit is what you would make if you sold now. Realised profit is what you actually made on coins you have already sold. Exchanges show both, and mixing them up leads to bad decisions.
