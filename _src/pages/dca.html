What dollar-cost averaging is

Dollar-cost averaging, or DCA, means buying a fixed amount of money's worth of a coin on a fixed schedule, whatever the price. When the price is low the same $50 buys more coins, and when it is high it buys fewer. Over time your average cost per coin ends up below the average price over the period, because more of your coins were bought on the cheap days.

Average cost = total money put in ÷ total coins bought

How this backtest works

The calculator loads one real closing price per day for the past year. Starting on the date you choose, it makes a buy at that day's price on every scheduled day, takes off the fee, and adds the coins to a running total. The final figure is those coins valued at today's price.

  1. Each buy converts your amount, minus the fee, into coins at that day's price.
  2. The chart tracks two lines: the money you have put in so far, and what your coins were worth on each day.
  3. The lump-sum row invests the same total in a single buy on the first day, so you can compare the two approaches on identical prices.

Nothing is simulated or smoothed. A calculator that asks only for a start and end price has to invent the path in between, and the path is the whole point of DCA.

A worked example

Three weekly buys of $100 at prices of $50,000, $40,000 and $60,000.

Coins: 0.00200 + 0.00250 + 0.00167 = 0.00617. Average cost: 300 ÷ 0.00617 = $48,649.

The plain average of the three prices is $50,000. DCA came in lower because the $40,000 week bought the most coins.

DCA or a lump sum?

Neither wins every time, and the comparison row will show you both outcomes. When a coin rises through the period, a lump sum on day one usually ends ahead, because all the money enjoyed the whole rise. When the price falls or swings sideways, DCA usually ends ahead, because later buys were cheaper than the first one.

The practical case for DCA is different. Most people do not have a lump sum, they have income that arrives monthly. And a schedule removes the decision about when to buy, which is the decision people most often get wrong.

Choosing how often to buy

Daily, weekly and monthly schedules land on similar average costs over a long period. The differences that matter are practical ones.

  • Fees: percentage fees cost the same however you split the money, but minimum or fixed fees hit small frequent buys hard. A $1 fixed fee on a $10 daily buy is 10%.
  • Timing risk: more buys spread the money across more prices. Twelve monthly buys can all land on bad days, while fifty-two weekly buys are less likely to.
  • Effort: an automatic recurring buy on your exchange costs no effort at any frequency. Doing it by hand is easiest monthly.

Limits of a backtest

  • The past is not a forecast. A plan that did well last year tells you nothing certain about next year.
  • One year of history. The free price feed provides 365 days of daily data, so longer plans cannot be tested here.
  • Daily prices. Your real buys fill at whatever the price is at that moment, which can differ from the daily figure by a percent or two.
  • No spread or tax. Only the percentage fee you enter is taken off.
