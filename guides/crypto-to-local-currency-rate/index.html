Why you get less than the converter shows

A converter shows the mid-market rate, the midpoint between what buyers bid and sellers ask. Nobody trades at exactly that price, and four separate costs sit between it and the money that reaches you.

The mid-market rate

Every market has two prices at once: the highest price a buyer will pay and the lowest price a seller will accept. The rate on a converter is the midpoint of the two, averaged across exchanges. It is the fairest single number for what a coin is worth, and a good yardstick for judging any offer you are given.

The four gaps

  • Spread. When you sell, you get the buyers' price, which is below the midpoint. On busy pairs the gap is tiny. On small coins or quiet local-currency markets it can be 1% or more.
  • Trading fee. The exchange takes a percentage of the trade, typically between 0.1% and 0.6%. See maker and taker fees.
  • Withdrawal cost. Sending the money to your bank or mobile wallet usually has a fixed charge, and your bank may add its own.
  • Network fee. If you first move the coins from a wallet or another exchange, the blockchain charges for the transfer. The cost varies greatly between networks, so check which networks both sides support before you send.

Adding it up

You sell 1,000 USDT for a currency that trades at 100 per USDT on the converter. The mid-market value is 100,000.

Spread of 0.3%: you sell at 99.70, giving 99,700

Trading fee of 0.1%: 99.70 comes off, leaving 99,600.30

Withdrawal charge of 50: 99,550.30 reaches your account

That is 0.45% below the converter figure. On small amounts the fixed charges weigh more: the same 50 withdrawal fee on a 100 USDT sale would be half a percent by itself.

Peer-to-peer prices

In many countries the usual way to turn crypto into local money is a peer-to-peer market, where you trade directly with another person and the platform holds the coins in escrow until payment is confirmed. Prices there are set by each seller and buyer, not by the global market.

  • The rate can sit above or below the official one. Where demand for dollars is high or bank transfers abroad are restricted, stablecoins often trade at a premium to the official exchange rate.
  • Each payment method has its own price. Offers for one bank or wallet can differ from another by a noticeable margin.
  • Compare the total, not the headline. A slightly better rate from a trader with few completed orders may not be worth the risk or the wait.

Keep the whole trade inside the platform's chat and escrow, and release coins only after the money shows in your own account. A screenshot of a transfer is not proof of payment.

How to keep more of it

  • Use the trading screen with a limit order instead of an instant-sell button, which usually hides a wider spread.
  • Make fewer, larger withdrawals when the charge is a fixed amount.
  • Check the rate against the converter before accepting an offer. A gap of more than a percent or two deserves a second look.

Common questions

Is the converter rate wrong if my exchange shows a different one?

No. The converter shows a market average. Your exchange shows its own buyers' and sellers' prices, which include its spread.

Why is the peer-to-peer rate higher than the official exchange rate?

Peer-to-peer prices follow local supply and demand for the coin. Where many people want stablecoins and few are selling, buyers pay more than the official rate.

Which costs the least, an exchange sale or peer-to-peer?

It varies by country and amount. Work out what reaches your account in each case, after every fee, and compare those two numbers.
