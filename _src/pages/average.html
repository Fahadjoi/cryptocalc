Why the average is not the middle of your prices

If you bought once at $60,000 and once at $40,000, it is tempting to call your average $50,000. That is only right if you bought the same number of coins each time. If you spent the same number of dollars each time, the cheaper buy gave you more coins, so your real average sits below the midpoint.

Average cost = total dollars spent ÷ total coins bought

A worked example

You spend $500 at $60,000 and another $500 at $40,000.

Coins: 500 ÷ 60,000 = 0.008333 and 500 ÷ 40,000 = 0.0125, which is 0.020833 in total.

Average cost: 1,000 ÷ 0.020833 = $48,000, not $50,000. Above $48,000 the whole position is in profit.

Your average cost is also your break-even price before fees. Selling everything above it makes money, and selling below it loses money, however the individual buys look.

Averaging down

Buying more after the price has fallen lowers your average cost, which means the price has less far to climb before you break even. The table of extra buys shows exactly how much each additional amount would move your average.

Coins to buy = (spent − target × coins held) ÷ (target − current price)Works when the target lies between your current average and the current price.

Two things are worth knowing before you do it. First, the average can only move toward the current price and never past it, so a target below today's price is impossible to reach by buying today. Second, the closer the target is to the current price, the more you have to buy. Moving your average halfway to the current price means doubling the number of coins you hold.

When averaging down helps and when it hurts

Averaging down improves your break-even price. It does not improve the coin. You end up with more money in the same asset, so if the price keeps falling the loss in dollars grows faster than before.

  • It suits planned buying of an asset you intended to accumulate anyway, with money set aside for that purpose.
  • It is risky when the reason for buying has changed, when it is done to avoid admitting a loss, or with leverage, where a larger position brings liquidation closer.

A useful check is to ask whether you would buy this coin today at this price if you held none. If the answer is no, a lower average is not a reason to buy more.

Fees and coins received

For the most accurate average, enter what each buy really cost and really delivered. In dollar mode, enter the full amount that left your account including the fee. In coin mode, enter the coins that arrived after the fee. The exchange's order history shows both.

Selling part of a position

Selling some coins does not change the average cost of the coins you keep. If you sell half at a profit, the remaining half still has the same average entry. Tax rules in your country may use a different method, such as first in, first out, so check those separately.

@faq Q: How do I calculate my average buy price in crypto? A: Add up all the money you spent and divide by the total number of coins you received. Spending $500 at $60,000 and $500 at $40,000 gives 0.020833 coins for $1,000, an average of $48,000. Q: What does averaging down mean? A: It means buying more of a coin after its price has dropped below your average cost. The new, cheaper coins pull your average down, so the price needs a smaller rise for you to break even. Q: How much do I need to buy to lower my average to a target? A: Enter the target in the calculator. The amount is your total spent minus the target times your coins, divided by the target minus the current price. The target has to sit between your present average and the current price. Q: Is my average cost the same as my break-even price? A: Yes, before selling fees. To cover a selling fee as well, the price needs to be slightly higher: divide your average by one minus the fee rate. Q: Does selling change my average cost? A: No. Selling reduces the number of coins you hold, but the coins that remain keep the same average entry price. @end
